# The Science of Fatigue: How Sleep Deprivation Changes Human Perception During Night Investigations

A sound comes from the far end of a corridor. An investigator turns, raises a recorder and hears what seems to be a whisper. A second team member reports seeing movement near a doorway. The group becomes alert: could this be an unexplained event?

But an important question may have been overlooked: **how much sleep has the team had?**

Night investigations often involve late hours, unfamiliar locations, long periods of waiting and heightened expectation. These conditions can make fatigue easy to dismiss as part of the experience. Yet sleep loss can affect attention, reaction time, memory, emotional regulation and the way ambiguous sensory information is interpreted.

A tired investigator may not be deliberately exaggerating an experience. They may be sincerely reporting what they believe they perceived. The challenge is that the brain's ability to notice, interpret and remember events can change when it is sleep-deprived.

For evidence-led paranormal research, fatigue is therefore not a minor inconvenience. It is a variable that must be documented and controlled.

This article examines what sleep deprivation can do to human perception, how those effects may influence field observations, and how an investigation team can reduce the risk of fatigue-related errors.

## 1. What Is Sleep Deprivation?

Sleep deprivation occurs when a person does not obtain enough sleep to meet their physiological needs. It may be acute, following one unusually short night or an extended period awake, or chronic, when a person repeatedly obtains insufficient sleep over days or weeks.

Sleep is an active biological process. It supports several functions important to investigation work, including:

- Maintaining attention and alertness.
- Supporting learning and memory consolidation.
- Regulating mood and emotional responses.
- Helping the brain process and integrate information.
- Supporting sound judgement, planning and decision-making.
- Maintaining normal reaction time and coordination.

The effects of sleep loss vary between individuals. They also depend on how much sleep was missed, how long the person has been awake, their recent sleep history, health, age, workload and the time of day.

A person may feel capable of continuing while their performance has already declined. Subjective confidence is not a reliable substitute for checking alertness and performance.

## 2. Why Night Investigations Can Increase Fatigue

A night investigation can combine several conditions that make fatigue more likely.

### Delayed or disrupted sleep schedules

Investigations may begin late in the evening and continue into the early morning. This can conflict with a person's usual sleep-wake rhythm. The body's circadian system influences alertness across the day, and many people experience a natural reduction in alertness during the biological night.

### Long periods of low activity

Waiting quietly in a dark room or monitoring equipment for extended periods can reduce stimulation. When a person is already tired, monotonous tasks may make it harder to sustain attention.

### Physical and mental workload

Carrying equipment, walking through a site, taking notes, monitoring multiple devices and evaluating reports all require effort. Even when physical activity is limited, sustained concentration can be mentally demanding.

### Environmental discomfort

Cold, heat, poor ventilation, uncomfortable seating and noise can make it harder to remain comfortable and alert. These conditions should be recorded rather than automatically attributed to an unusual cause.

### Excitement and expectation

An investigator may feel temporarily energised by anticipation. However, excitement does not reverse the cognitive effects of sleep loss. A person can feel highly engaged while still being less reliable at detecting details, remembering sequences or evaluating uncertain information.

## 3. How Sleep Loss Affects Attention

Attention is the ability to select relevant information and maintain focus. It is essential when monitoring audio, video, environmental instruments and witness activity.

Sleep deprivation can produce:

- **Reduced sustained attention:** difficulty maintaining focus over time.
- **Attention lapses:** brief periods in which a person fails to respond to information.
- **Slower reactions:** delayed responses to sounds, movement or instructions.
- **Greater distractibility:** difficulty ignoring irrelevant noises or activity.
- **Inconsistent performance:** periods of apparently normal alertness mixed with sudden lapses.

Imagine an investigator monitoring a fixed camera feed for forty minutes. A brief movement appears at the edge of the frame. If attention has lapsed, the investigator may miss the beginning or end of the event. Later, they may describe the movement from an incomplete observation.

This matters because an incomplete observation can become a misleading evidence claim. A recording may show only a fragment of the event, while the observer's memory supplies details that were not clearly seen.

**Field implication:** long monitoring periods should be divided into manageable shifts, with active handovers and breaks. Critical observations should be time-stamped as they happen rather than reconstructed from memory later.

## 4. Perception Under Fatigue: When Ambiguous Information Becomes Difficult to Interpret

Human perception is not a simple recording process. The brain combines incoming sensory information with context, expectation, prior experience and attention to form an interpretation.

In low-light or noisy environments, sensory information may be incomplete. Fatigue can make the task of evaluating that information more difficult.

Consider a few common investigation situations:

### A shape in a dark doorway

A coat, hanging cable or uneven wall surface may create a shape that resembles a person. In darkness, visual detail is limited. An exhausted observer who is already expecting a presence may be more likely to settle quickly on a human-like interpretation.

### A sound that resembles a voice

A distant conversation, air movement, an animal call, pipe noise or electronic interference may contain patterns that resemble speech. When the recording is unclear, a listener may perceive a familiar word or phrase.

### Movement in peripheral vision

Peripheral vision is useful for detecting movement but provides less detail than direct central vision. A brief shadow or change in lighting may be noticed without enough information to identify its source.

These examples do not mean that every unusual report is caused by fatigue. They demonstrate why an observation should be separated from the explanation assigned to it.

A useful field note distinguishes:

1. **Observation:** "A dark shape was visible near the left doorway for approximately two seconds."
2. **Interpretation:** "The observer thought the shape resembled a person."
3. **Verification:** "The team reviewed the camera footage, checked the doorway and compared the event with lighting and equipment conditions."

This separation protects the integrity of the record.

## 5. Memory and the Reconstruction of Events

Memory is not a perfect video archive. People encode, store and retrieve information, and each stage can be affected by attention, stress, expectation and sleep.

Sleep supports memory processing and consolidation. Insufficient sleep can impair the ability to learn and retain new information. During a long investigation, this can affect the accuracy of later recollections.

For example, after several hours awake, a witness may remember that a sound occurred but be uncertain about its exact timing, direction or sequence. If team members discuss their interpretations before writing independent accounts, one person's description may influence another person's memory.

This is especially important when several investigators report the same event. Agreement is useful information, but it does not automatically prove that each person independently perceived the same details. Shared discussion, common expectations and environmental cues can influence reports.

### A practical memory protocol

- Ask each witness to write an account independently before group discussion.
- Record the time of the event as accurately as possible.
- Use neutral questions such as "What did you notice?" rather than "Did you hear a voice?"
- Preserve the witness's original wording.
- Separate what was directly perceived from what was inferred.
- Record uncertainty instead of forcing a precise answer.
- Compare accounts only after independent statements have been secured.

## 6. Fatigue, Emotion and Expectation

Sleep loss can affect emotional regulation. People may become more irritable, emotionally reactive or less able to manage frustration. Some may also find it harder to evaluate a situation calmly.

In a reportedly haunted location, the atmosphere may already be intense. Darkness, unfamiliar sounds, isolation and stories about the site can raise expectation. Fatigue can add another source of uncertainty.

A sudden noise might trigger a stronger startle response. A harmless environmental change may feel threatening. An investigator who is anxious may interpret an ambiguous event as confirmation of an existing suspicion.

This is one reason investigators should avoid leading questions and dramatic suggestions during evidence collection. Statements such as "Something is standing behind you" can influence attention and expectation, potentially changing what people report.

A neutral approach is more reliable:

- "Did you notice anything?"
- "Describe what happened in your own words."
- "Where were you looking at the time?"
- "How certain are you about what you observed?"
- "Was anyone else speaking or moving?"

The objective is not to dismiss the witness. It is to collect an account without unnecessarily shaping it.

## 7. Severe Sleep Loss and Unusual Experiences

With substantial sleep deprivation, some people may experience perceptual disturbances, including visual misperceptions, unusual sensory experiences or, in more severe cases, hallucinations. The likelihood and nature of these effects vary, and they should not be treated as inevitable consequences of one late night.

It is important to distinguish ordinary fatigue from severe sleep loss. A person who has been awake for an extended period may be unsafe to continue investigating, driving or operating equipment.

If a team member reports seeing or hearing something unusual while severely sleep-deprived, the correct response is not to label the event paranormal. The team should stop the activity, ensure the person's safety, document relevant circumstances and allow appropriate rest. Persistent, distressing or recurrent perceptual experiences should be discussed with a qualified healthcare professional.

An investigation must never deliberately deprive participants of sleep to provoke unusual experiences. That would create an avoidable safety risk and undermine the reliability of the evidence.

## 8. Why Fatigue Can Affect Instrument-Based Evidence

Fatigue does not only affect witness testimony. It can also influence how investigators operate and interpret equipment.

### Audio recorders

A tired operator may forget to note handling noise, microphone position, nearby conversation or changes in recorder settings. Later, an ambiguous sound may be interpreted without the contextual information needed to evaluate it.

### EMF meters

An investigator may become less consistent in recording baseline readings, device orientation, distance from electrical sources or changes in the surrounding environment. A fluctuating reading is not meaningful without context and repeatable measurement.

### Thermal cameras

A fatigued operator may overlook reflections, emissivity settings, environmental changes or the difference between apparent surface temperature and air temperature. A thermal image should be interpreted using the device's limitations and environmental context.

### Video equipment

A tired operator may fail to record a camera adjustment, light source, exposure change or movement near the lens. These omissions can make later review more difficult.

### Field notes and timestamps

Fatigue can lead to incomplete notes, incorrect times, missed observations and inconsistent terminology. These errors weaken the chain of documentation.

Equipment does not automatically make an observation objective. The person operating it remains part of the measurement process.

## 9. Fatigue as a Confounding Variable in Paranormal Research

A confounding variable is a factor that can influence the observations or results of an investigation, making it harder to identify what caused an event.

If unusual reports occur mainly after several hours of continuous night work, fatigue should be considered as one possible contributing factor. It may not explain every event, but ignoring it leaves an important alternative explanation untested.

AERM recommends recording fatigue-related information alongside environmental and technical observations.

| Variable | What to record | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Previous night's sleep | Approximate hours and quality | Establishes recent sleep history |
| Time awake | Approximate time since waking | Helps assess accumulated wakefulness |
| Investigation time | Start, end and event timestamps | Identifies late-night patterns |
| Shift duration | Time spent on each task | Reveals prolonged monitoring periods |
| Breaks | Timing and duration | Documents recovery opportunities |
| Self-reported alertness | A consistent rating scale | Adds context to witness reports |
| Caffeine or other stimulants | Type, amount and timing, if voluntarily disclosed | Provides context without assuming they eliminate fatigue |
| Unusual experiences | Exact description and time | Allows comparison with fatigue and task conditions |

This information should be collected respectfully and with appropriate privacy safeguards. Participants should not be pressured to disclose medical information.

## 10. A Practical Fatigue-Control Protocol for Investigation Teams

A professional investigation should be designed so that reliable work does not depend on people pushing through exhaustion.

### Before the investigation

1. **Set a reasonable schedule.** Avoid planning an unnecessarily long overnight session.
2. **Ask participants to arrive rested.** Explain that the team may postpone or shorten work if someone is unfit to participate.
3. **Assign roles in advance.** Avoid making one person responsible for continuous monitoring, note-taking and equipment operation.
4. **Prepare equipment before the session.** Reduce avoidable workload during late hours.
5. **Establish a stop-work rule.** Every participant should be able to pause the investigation without pressure.

### During the investigation

1. **Use rotating shifts.** Share monitoring duties and avoid extended periods of monotonous observation.
2. **Schedule breaks.** Provide opportunities to sit, hydrate, eat appropriately and rest.
3. **Record observations immediately.** Use timestamps and neutral descriptions.
4. **Check alertness.** Ask participants privately and regularly whether they feel able to continue safely.
5. **Avoid leading discussion.** Secure independent witness accounts before comparing interpretations.
6. **Do not use stimulants as a substitute for sleep.** Caffeine may temporarily increase alertness for some people, but it does not restore all functions affected by sleep deprivation.
7. **Stop when performance becomes unreliable.** Confusion, repeated attention lapses, microsleeps, poor coordination or difficulty following instructions are reasons to end the person's participation.

### After the investigation

1. **Do not conduct critical evidence review while exhausted.** Preserve the original files and schedule detailed analysis after adequate rest.
2. **Keep original notes.** Do not silently rewrite an account after group discussion.
3. **Record the team's condition.** Include shift lengths, breaks and any fatigue-related concerns in the session report.
4. **Review possible confounders.** Compare event times with sleep history, environmental readings and equipment logs.
5. **Arrange safe transport.** A person who is sleepy should not drive. Use a rested driver or another safe transport option.

## 11. A Simple AERM Fatigue Observation Scale

A consistent self-report scale can help a team document alertness. It is not a medical assessment and should never be used to certify that someone is safe to continue.

| Rating | Description | Suggested field response |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Fully alert and attentive | Normal duties, with routine breaks |
| 2 | Slightly tired but functioning normally | Monitor and maintain scheduled breaks |
| 3 | Noticeably tired; concentration takes effort | Rotate tasks and take a rest break |
| 4 | Very tired; repeated difficulty maintaining attention | Stop critical monitoring and reassess participation |
| 5 | Struggling to stay awake, confused or experiencing microsleeps | Stop participation; arrange safe rest and transport |

The scale should be completed honestly and without judgement. A participant should never be penalised for reporting fatigue. The aim is to protect people and improve evidence quality, not to encourage them to endure unsafe conditions.

## 12. Example: Reviewing a Late-Night Voice Report

Consider a hypothetical investigation in which two team members report hearing a faint voice at 3:10 a.m.

A weak report might say:

> "At 3:10 a.m., a spirit whispered near the staircase."

This statement combines a time, an interpretation and a conclusion without showing how the conclusion was reached.

A stronger record would be:

- **Time:** 03:10, based on the synchronized recorder clock.
- **Observer:** Investigator A, positioned approximately four metres from the staircase.
- **Direct observation:** "I heard a short, low-volume sound that reminded me of a human voice."
- **Confidence:** The observer was uncertain about the words and direction.
- **Fatigue context:** The observer reported approximately five hours of sleep the previous night and had been awake for about twenty hours.
- **Technical record:** Original audio file preserved; recorder placement, gain settings and nearby equipment documented.
- **Environmental checks:** Team conversation, nearby occupants, road noise, ventilation and other plausible sound sources checked where possible.
- **Independent account:** Other team members recorded their observations before hearing the first investigator's interpretation.
- **Review outcome:** The sound was retained as an unexplained or unidentified audio event only if the available evidence did not establish its source. It was not classified as paranormal merely because it resembled speech.

The fatigue information does not prove that the sound was imagined. It changes how confidently the witness account can be interpreted and identifies a factor that should be considered during review.

## 13. Ethical Considerations: Never Treat Exhaustion as an Experiment

Sleep deprivation can impair judgement and increase safety risks. It should not be used as a paranormal investigation technique.

Teams should not:

- Encourage participants to remain awake for extreme periods.
- Deliberately interrupt sleep to provoke unusual experiences.
- Pressure a tired participant to continue.
- Treat confusion or distress as evidence of paranormal activity.
- Allow a sleepy person to drive or operate hazardous equipment.
- Publish a participant's health or sleep information without appropriate consent.

If someone becomes disoriented, unusually distressed or unable to remain awake, end their participation and prioritise their wellbeing. Seek medical assistance when symptoms are severe, persistent or otherwise concerning.

Research involving human participants should follow informed-consent, privacy and risk-management principles. A dramatic investigation is never more important than participant safety.

## 14. What Sleep Deprivation Does Not Prove

Careful research requires restraint. Fatigue can affect attention, memory, emotional regulation and perception, but it does not automatically invalidate every report made at night.

It would be just as unscientific to dismiss every unusual experience as fatigue as it would be to label every ambiguous event paranormal.

The correct questions are:

- Was the participant adequately rested?
- How long had they been awake?
- Was the observation made during a monotonous or demanding task?
- Was the event independently observed or recorded?
- Were the original files preserved?
- Were environmental and technical explanations evaluated?
- Could expectation or group discussion have influenced the account?
- Can the observation be repeated under controlled conditions?

The conclusion should match the strength of the evidence. If the source cannot be established, "unidentified" is more accurate than an unsupported paranormal label.

## 15. AERM Research Framework: Include the Human Observer in the Investigation

At AERM, the investigator is not separate from the investigation environment. Human attention, memory, expectation, physical condition and equipment-handling practices all influence the information collected.

A fatigue-aware investigation therefore includes three layers of review:

### Human factors

- Sleep duration and recent sleep quality, where voluntarily provided.
- Time awake and shift duration.
- Alertness and concentration.
- Stress, discomfort and task demands.
- Independent witness statements.

### Environmental and technical factors

- Lighting and visibility.
- Ambient sound and possible sound sources.
- Temperature, ventilation and air quality where relevant.
- Electrical sources and instrument limitations.
- Camera, microphone and sensor placement.

### Evidence integrity

- Original recordings and metadata.
- Synchronized timestamps.
- Contemporaneous field notes.
- Documented equipment settings.
- Independent review and transparent uncertainty.

The purpose is not to eliminate human judgement; that is impossible. The purpose is to understand its limits and reduce avoidable sources of error.

## Conclusion: A Rested Investigator Is a More Reliable Investigator

Night investigations can be compelling, but the conditions that make them atmospheric can also make them difficult to evaluate. Sleep deprivation may reduce sustained attention, slow reaction time, affect memory and make ambiguous information harder to interpret. These effects can influence both witness reports and the quality of equipment operation.

Fatigue should therefore be documented as part of the investigation record. Teams should use reasonable schedules, rotating duties, planned breaks, independent witness accounts and clear stop-work rules. Evidence should be reviewed after adequate rest, with original recordings and notes preserved.

Most importantly, investigators should never confuse endurance with professionalism. Continuing while exhausted does not make an investigation more scientific. Recognising when human performance is becoming unreliable is itself a sign of good research practice.

**AERM's guiding principle remains:**

> No anomaly is considered paranormal until environmental, psychological, technical, and scientific explanations have been systematically evaluated.

That evaluation must include the condition of the people collecting the evidence.
