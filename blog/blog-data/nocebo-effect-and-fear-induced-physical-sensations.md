# The Nocebo Effect: Can Fear and Expectation Create Real Physical Sensations?

A person enters an abandoned building after hearing that visitors often experience sudden chills. A few minutes later, they report a cold sensation along their arms. Another person is told that a particular room causes headaches. Before long, they begin to notice pressure around their temples.

Were the sensations real? Yes. Does that automatically mean the building produced an unusual physical effect? No.

Human experience is shaped not only by what happens around us, but also by what we expect to happen. In some circumstances, negative expectations can contribute to genuine symptoms such as pain, nausea, dizziness, itching, fatigue, or headaches. This phenomenon is known as the **nocebo effect**.

The nocebo effect is especially relevant to paranormal investigations, where stories, rumours, frightening locations, and expectations can influence what people notice and how they interpret it.

Understanding the effect does not mean accusing witnesses of imagining symptoms. It means recognising that expectation and context can affect the body, and that a careful investigation should account for these influences.

## 1. What Is the Nocebo Effect?

The term *nocebo* comes from Latin and is commonly translated as “I shall harm.” In health research, the **nocebo effect** describes negative outcomes or symptoms that arise or become worse in association with negative expectations, learning, or the context surrounding an experience.

For example, a person who is warned that a harmless procedure may cause discomfort might report more discomfort than someone who receives neutral information. In some cases, a person may experience symptoms after being told that an inactive substance or harmless exposure could produce them.

The key point is that symptoms can be genuine even when they are not caused by the specific harmful agent a person expects.

The nocebo effect is often discussed alongside the placebo effect:

- **Placebo effect:** positive expectations and treatment context may contribute to beneficial changes in symptoms or experience.
- **Nocebo effect:** negative expectations and treatment context may contribute to unpleasant symptoms or worsening of symptoms.

Neither term means that a person's experience is fake. Both concern the ways expectation, learning, attention, and context can interact with bodily processes.

## 2. Can Expectation Produce Real Physical Sensations?

Yes. Research indicates that expectations can influence the perception of symptoms and, in some circumstances, physiological responses.

Imagine two people entering the same quiet room. One has been told that the room is harmless and well ventilated. The other has been told that previous visitors experienced nausea and breathing discomfort there.

They may notice different things. The second person may monitor their breathing more closely, interpret ordinary bodily sensations as warning signs, and become increasingly anxious. Anxiety itself can produce physical changes, including a faster heartbeat, muscle tension, sweating, and altered breathing.

This does not mean that expectation is the only possible explanation. Poor ventilation, an actual odour, temperature, illness, medication, or another environmental factor may also be relevant. The nocebo effect is one possible contributor that should be considered alongside other explanations.

### Symptoms that may be influenced

Depending on the person and situation, negative expectations and heightened attention may contribute to reports of:

- Headache or head pressure.
- Nausea or stomach discomfort.
- Dizziness or light-headedness.
- Itching or tingling.
- Muscle tension or pain.
- Fatigue.
- Breathlessness or a feeling of difficulty breathing.
- Increased awareness of heartbeat.
- General discomfort or a sense that something is wrong.

These symptoms are not specific to the nocebo effect. They can have many medical, environmental, and psychological causes. A list of symptoms cannot establish the mechanism behind an individual case.

## 3. How Does the Nocebo Effect Work?

There is no single mechanism that explains every nocebo response. Research points to several interacting processes, including expectation, learning, attention, anxiety, and the interpretation of bodily signals.

### 3.1 Negative expectation

What a person expects can influence what they notice and how they interpret it.

If someone is told that a building causes sudden headaches, they may become especially alert to minor sensations around the head. A sensation that would otherwise pass unnoticed may become prominent and concerning.

Expectation does not create every symptom from nothing. It can change the threshold at which a sensation is noticed, the meaning assigned to it, and how distressing it feels.

### 3.2 Conditioning and learned associations

People learn from previous experiences. If a person repeatedly feels unwell in a particular setting, they may begin to associate that place with discomfort.

Later, entering the same setting may trigger anticipatory anxiety or bodily responses before any new environmental cause has been established.

This process is related to classical conditioning: a context that has become associated with an unpleasant experience can begin to trigger a learned response.

The association may be accurate, partly accurate, or mistaken. Investigators need evidence to determine whether a repeatable environmental factor is present.

### 3.3 Attention to bodily sensations

The body constantly produces signals: changes in temperature, muscle tension, digestion, breathing, balance, and heartbeat.

Most of these signals are not consciously examined all the time. However, when a person expects danger, attention may become focused on the body.

A normal variation in breathing may feel unusual. A small muscle twitch may seem significant. A mild headache may become the centre of attention.

Increased attention can make a sensation more noticeable and may contribute to distress. It does not prove that the sensation has no physical basis.

### 3.4 Anxiety and the stress response

Fear can activate the body's stress response. This may involve changes in heart rate, breathing, sweating, muscle tension, and alertness.

For example:

1. A person hears that a location is dangerous.
2. They enter the location expecting something unpleasant.
3. They become more alert to sounds and bodily sensations.
4. Anxiety increases.
5. Their heartbeat, breathing, or muscle tension changes.
6. These changes may be interpreted as evidence that something is wrong in the room.

This can create a feedback loop in which fear increases bodily sensations, and the sensations then increase fear.

### 3.5 Interpretation and symptom amplification

A physical sensation does not arrive with an explanation attached. People interpret it using context, memory, beliefs, and available information.

A sudden chill might be attributed to an air-conditioning vent, damp clothing, or a supernatural presence. A headache might be linked to dehydration, stress, poor lighting, or a rumoured “negative energy” in a room.

The sensation may be real, while the explanation remains uncertain.

## 4. The Nocebo Effect in Paranormal Settings

Paranormal investigations often take place in environments that encourage expectation: abandoned buildings, old houses, isolated corridors, cemeteries, or locations with dramatic histories.

Before entering, team members may have heard stories about apparitions, unexplained noises, previous visitors becoming ill, or a particular room being associated with frightening events.

This background information can shape the investigation in several ways.

### The influence of location stories

A room described as “the cold room” may attract more attention to temperature changes. A corridor associated with footsteps may make ordinary building sounds seem more significant.

### Group expectations

If one team member says, “I feel something watching us,” others may begin to monitor the same area for signs of a presence.

### Suggestive questions

Questions such as “Do you feel pressure on your chest?” or “Are you getting a headache now?” can direct attention toward specific sensations.

### Repeated interpretation

If every unexplained sound is described as paranormal, team members may become more likely to interpret later ambiguous events in the same way.

These influences do not demonstrate that every report is caused by suggestion. They show why investigators should record what was noticed before discussing possible explanations.

## 5. Nocebo, Fear, and the Difference Between Sensation and Explanation

A useful investigation separates three layers of information.

| Layer | Example | What it tells us |
|---|---|---|
| Sensation | “I felt pressure around my chest.” | What the person experienced |
| Interpretation | “Something was pressing on me.” | How the person understood the sensation |
| Evidence | A time-stamped observation, environmental reading, or independent corroboration | What can be examined beyond the interpretation |

Consider this example:

A visitor enters a room after being told that it is associated with a frightening presence. The visitor reports tingling in both hands and a racing heartbeat.

The symptoms should be documented as reported. The investigator can then ask when they began, whether the visitor had experienced them before, and whether there were relevant environmental or health factors.

The investigator should not write “an entity caused tingling” unless there is evidence that supports such a conclusion. Nor should they write “the visitor imagined it.” Neither statement is justified by the report alone.

A more careful entry would be:

> The visitor reported tingling in both hands and an increased awareness of heartbeat after entering the room. The symptoms were self-reported. The timing, environmental conditions, and possible contribution of expectation or anxiety require further assessment. No cause was established during the session.

## 6. The Role of Suggestion: How Investigators Can Accidentally Influence Witnesses

An investigator can unintentionally shape a witness's report through wording, tone, body language, or repeated questioning.

Compare these questions:

**Leading question:** “You felt a cold presence near the window, didn't you?”

**Neutral question:** “Did you notice any change in temperature? If so, describe what you noticed and where.”

The first question suggests both a sensation and an interpretation. The second allows the witness to report what they actually noticed.

Other examples of potentially suggestive prompts include:

- “Did you hear that whisper?”
- “Are you feeling watched?”
- “Does this room make you feel sick?”
- “Can you sense someone standing behind you?”

More neutral alternatives include:

- “What, if anything, did you notice?”
- “Can you describe the sound in your own words?”
- “Did you experience any physical sensation?”
- “When did you first notice it?”
- “What were you doing immediately beforehand?”

The goal is not to avoid discussing unusual experiences. It is to avoid supplying details that the witness has not independently reported.

## 7. How to Design an Investigation That Reduces Nocebo and Suggestion

AERM investigations should be designed to reduce avoidable expectation effects while still collecting useful information.

### Step 1: Record expectations before entering

Ask team members what they have heard about the location and what they expect to experience. Record this before the investigation begins.

This creates a reference point for later comparison. It may help identify whether a reported sensation was anticipated before it occurred.

### Step 2: Use neutral briefings

Provide necessary safety information, but avoid dramatic descriptions of alleged paranormal activity immediately before a session.

If historical accounts must be discussed, consider documenting them separately and sharing them with investigators only after an initial observation period. This can help reduce the influence of location stories on first impressions.

### Step 3: Collect independent observations

Where practical, ask team members to record their observations separately before discussing them.

For example, if three investigators report hearing a sound, collect each account independently. Then compare the descriptions, timestamps, and directions.

Independent notes are not automatically independent evidence if team members have already influenced one another. Record whether discussion occurred before the reports were written.

### Step 4: Use pre-defined observation periods

Decide in advance how long a monitoring period will last, where instruments will be placed, and what will be recorded.

Avoid changing the procedure only because a team member reports a sensation. Any changes should be documented with the reason and time.

### Step 5: Record physical measurements separately

If someone reports a cold sensation, record the person's report separately from instrument readings.

For example:

- Witness report: “My left arm felt cold.”
- Instrument observation: “The temperature sensor recorded 22.4°C at the marked position.”
- Context: “A ventilation outlet was located approximately two metres away.”

A temperature reading does not measure the person's subjective sensation. A subjective sensation does not establish that the room's temperature changed.

### Step 6: Document uncertainty and alternative explanations

Include possible influences such as fatigue, stress, expectation, recent physical activity, medication where voluntarily disclosed, lighting, ventilation, noise, and temperature.

Do not assume any one factor explains the event without adequate support.

## 8. A Simple AERM Nocebo-Awareness Protocol

The following protocol is a practical documentation framework, not a clinical test or a validated diagnostic scale.

**Before the session**

- Record what each investigator knows about the location.
- Ask what, if anything, they expect to experience.
- Record sleep, fatigue, and general comfort where relevant and voluntarily disclosed.
- Explain that investigators should report observations without assigning a cause.

**During the session**

- Use neutral language.
- Record the time and location of each reported sensation.
- Ask the witness to describe the sensation without suggesting a specific symptom.
- Record environmental measurements independently.
- Avoid discussing interpretations until individual observations have been documented.

**After the session**

- Compare independent notes.
- Identify which details were recorded before group discussion.
- Review whether expectations may have influenced attention or interpretation.
- Check environmental and technical explanations.
- State what remains unknown.

### Example observation record

| Field | Example entry |
|---|---|
| Session ID | AERM-NS-01 |
| Time | 22:35 (local time) |
| Location | Room B, east side |
| Witness report | Tingling in both hands |
| First noticed | Approximately two minutes after entering |
| Prior expectation | Witness had heard the room was associated with unusual sensations |
| Environmental readings | Record actual instrument readings; do not estimate |
| Other observations | Record independently reported sounds, airflow, lighting, or activity |
| Interpretation | Not assigned during initial observation |
| Follow-up | Compare with independent notes and environmental records |

Do not use this example as a real measurement record. Replace illustrative details with observations actually collected during a session.

## 9. What the Nocebo Effect Does Not Explain

The nocebo effect is important, but it should not become a convenient explanation for every uncomfortable or unexplained event.

It does not establish that:

- Every headache in an allegedly haunted building is expectation-driven.
- Every report of nausea or dizziness is psychological.
- Environmental hazards can be ignored.
- Witnesses are unreliable whenever they are frightened.
- A physical symptom has no medical significance.
- An investigator can identify the cause of a symptom from a short interview.

A location may contain real hazards, including poor air quality, carbon monoxide, mould, chemical exposure, unsafe temperatures, or inadequate ventilation. These possibilities must be assessed independently.

**Safety comes first.** If anyone experiences severe headache, confusion, fainting, chest pain, significant breathing difficulty, or multiple people become unwell in the same location, stop the activity, move to fresh air if it is safe to do so, and seek appropriate emergency or medical assistance. Suspected carbon monoxide exposure requires immediate attention; do not re-enter a potentially contaminated building to continue an investigation.

## 10. How Is the Nocebo Effect Studied?

Researchers use controlled experiments to investigate how expectation and context influence symptoms.

A simplified experimental design may compare groups that receive different information about an otherwise similar experience. Researchers can then measure self-reported symptoms and, where appropriate, physiological responses.

However, ethical research must avoid unnecessary harm. Participants should receive appropriate information, consent procedures, and safeguards. Researchers must not expose people to genuine hazards under the guise of studying expectation.

Nocebo research also faces methodological challenges:

- People differ in their expectations and prior experiences.
- Symptoms are often measured through self-report.
- Participants may guess the purpose of an experiment.
- Context and communication can be difficult to standardise.
- A reported symptom may have more than one contributing cause.

The results of a controlled study should not be used to diagnose an individual witness or to claim that a particular paranormal report has been explained.

## 11. Evidence, Ethics, and Respect for Witnesses

The nocebo effect can be misunderstood in two opposite ways.

One mistake is to treat every symptom as proof that an external force acted on the person. The other is to use psychology as a way to dismiss the witness.

Both approaches are inadequate.

A witness can experience real pain, nausea, fear, or dizziness while being mistaken about the cause. An investigator can acknowledge the symptom without endorsing an unsupported explanation.

Ethical documentation should:

- Use the witness's own words where possible.
- Avoid ridicule, confrontation, or suggestive questioning.
- Distinguish direct observation from interpretation.
- Obtain consent before recording or sharing personal information.
- Avoid publishing identifiable health details without permission.
- Never encourage someone to remain in an unsafe environment to collect evidence.
- Avoid medical diagnosis unless the investigator is appropriately qualified and acting within their professional role.

The purpose of investigation is to understand an event as accurately as possible, not to win an argument about belief.

## 12. AERM Research Perspective

For AERM, the nocebo effect is an important consideration in any investigation involving reported physical sensations.

A person may feel a sudden chill, headache, pressure, tingling, or nausea in a location associated with frightening stories. These reports deserve accurate documentation. At the same time, expectation, attention, anxiety, environmental conditions, and health factors should be considered before a cause is assigned.

AERM's approach is to separate what a witness experienced from what they believe caused it. Investigators should use neutral questions, independent records, appropriate measurements, and clear statements of uncertainty.

> **No anomaly is considered paranormal until environmental, psychological, technical, and scientific explanations have been systematically evaluated.**

The nocebo effect does not prove that a reported event is psychological. It reminds us that the human response is part of the investigation and must be considered with the same care as the building, equipment, and environment.

## Conclusion

The nocebo effect demonstrates that negative expectations and context can contribute to genuine physical symptoms. Fear can change attention, influence the interpretation of bodily signals, and activate stress-related responses. In an atmosphere shaped by rumours or frightening expectations, ordinary sensations may become unusually noticeable or distressing.

For paranormal investigators, this is not a reason to dismiss witnesses. It is a reason to improve the quality of observation.

Record the sensation. Ask neutral questions. Check the environment. Keep independent notes. Consider health and safety. Separate the witness's experience from the explanation offered for it.

An evidence-based investigation does not begin by deciding what an event must mean. It begins by documenting what actually happened—and being honest about what is still unknown.
