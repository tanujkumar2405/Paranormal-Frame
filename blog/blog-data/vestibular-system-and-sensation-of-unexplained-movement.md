## Introduction

People sometimes report that a stationary floor feels as though it is moving, that a room tilts, or that they are being pulled or rotated when nothing nearby appears to move. Such experiences can be unsettling, particularly in dim buildings or unfamiliar locations. The human balance system is complex: it combines information from the inner ear, eyes, muscles and joints to estimate body position and movement.

The vestibular system does not simply detect “motion.” It helps the brain estimate head rotation, linear acceleration and orientation relative to gravity. When sensory signals conflict, are reduced or are interpreted in an unusual context, a person may experience dizziness, imbalance or an illusion of movement.

## The vestibular system

The peripheral vestibular organs are located in the inner ear. They include:

- **Semicircular canals:** three approximately perpendicular canals detect angular head acceleration. Fluid movement deflects sensory hair cells.
- **Otolith organs:** the utricle and saccule detect linear acceleration and head orientation relative to gravity through tiny calcium-carbonate crystals called otoconia.
- **Vestibular nerve pathways:** carry signals to brainstem and cerebellar networks.
- **Central integration:** the brain combines vestibular information with visual and somatosensory signals to guide posture, gaze and spatial orientation.

The vestibulo-ocular reflex helps stabilise vision when the head moves. If the head turns, the eyes move in the opposite direction to keep a target relatively stable.

## Sensory conflict and motion perception

Balance depends on agreement among several sources of information. Imagine standing in a dark room: visual landmarks are limited, so the brain relies more heavily on inner-ear and body-position signals. On a moving platform, visual and vestibular signals may disagree. In some settings, this mismatch can contribute to dizziness or a feeling of movement.

Examples include:

- **Visual dependence:** large moving visual patterns can create a sense of self-motion.
- **Motion aftereffects:** after travelling in a moving vehicle, a stationary environment may briefly feel unusual.
- **Postural sway:** everyone makes small balance corrections, even while standing still.
- **Vestibular disorders:** certain conditions can produce vertigo, imbalance or motion sensitivity.

Vertigo is a symptom—a false sensation of movement—not a diagnosis by itself. Causes vary and require clinical assessment.

## Why an investigation environment can matter

Uneven floors, sloping corridors, low lighting, repetitive wall patterns, moving shadows and lack of distant visual references can influence orientation. A person who is tired, dehydrated or anxious may also find it harder to interpret ambiguous bodily sensations. These factors should be documented, not assumed to explain every report.

A reported sensation should be described in the witness's own words. “The floor moved sideways for several seconds” is more useful than immediately labelling it a paranormal push.

## AERM field protocol

### Before a session

- Inspect floors, stairs, ramps and loose surfaces.
- Record lighting levels and the availability of stable visual references.
- Identify moving machinery, vibration sources and nearby traffic.
- Provide clear walking routes and avoid dark-room movement tests.
- Ask participants to disclose relevant safety needs voluntarily; do not request unnecessary medical details.

### If someone reports movement

1. Stop the activity and help the person sit or stand safely.
2. Ask neutrally what they experienced, how long it lasted and whether it recurred.
3. Record location, posture, lighting, recent movement and environmental conditions.
4. Do not spin, push, blindfold or deliberately provoke dizziness.
5. Check whether other people independently report the same event, without leading them.
6. If the person feels unwell, end their participation and arrange appropriate assistance.

### Environmental checks

Use a level to document floor slope, a vibration logger where appropriate, and a light meter to characterise illumination. These tools do not diagnose a vestibular condition. They document environmental variables that may be relevant to a report.

## What investigators must not do

Do not interpret dizziness as evidence of a presence. Do not conduct improvised balance challenges, induce vertigo or ask participants to walk through hazards with their eyes closed. A sudden severe episode of dizziness, especially with weakness, difficulty speaking, severe headache, chest pain, fainting or new neurological symptoms, requires urgent medical attention.

## Conclusion

The vestibular system is a sophisticated motion and orientation network. Its signals are integrated with vision and body sensation, and mismatches can create powerful movement sensations even when the surroundings appear stationary. In field research, the responsible approach is to document the experience, assess the environment and avoid medical speculation. A sensory report is important evidence about what a person experienced; it is not, by itself, evidence of an external agent.
