# Understanding Sensors: Accuracy, Precision, Resolution and Calibration

A sensor can display many decimal places and still be wrong. Understanding measurement quality is therefore essential before using environmental instruments in research. Four terms are especially important: accuracy, precision, resolution and calibration.

**Accuracy** describes closeness to an accepted reference value. **Precision** describes how closely repeated measurements agree with one another. **Resolution** is the smallest change an instrument can display or distinguish. **Calibration** compares an instrument's response with reference standards under defined conditions. These properties are related but not interchangeable.

A thermometer that repeatedly reads 1.5 °C too high may be precise but inaccurate. A meter that jumps between values may have poor repeatability. A display showing 0.001 units does not prove that the instrument can measure to that level. The number of digits is not the same as measurement capability.

**Uncertainty matters.** Every measurement has uncertainty from the instrument, environment, operator, placement and method. A reading should therefore be reported with units, instrument model, range, calibration information and relevant conditions. Where a manufacturer specifies accuracy as ± a value, include that limitation rather than reporting a bare number.

**Calibration and checks.** Follow manufacturer instructions and use traceable reference standards when the measurement requires them. A quick check against a household device is not a formal calibration. Record calibration dates and any adjustment. If an instrument is dropped, exposed to moisture or used outside its specified range, reassess its condition.

**Field exercise.** Take repeated measurements at a stable location without moving the sensor. Calculate the range and average, then repeat after repositioning it. Compare the observed variation with the stated instrument specifications. If possible, compare against a suitable reference instrument. Document differences rather than selecting whichever reading supports the preferred explanation.

**Why this matters in anomalous research.** A sensor spike may be caused by a real environmental change, electrical interference, sensor drift or handling. Without knowing the instrument's response, investigators cannot distinguish these possibilities confidently. A sensor does not interpret its own data.

**Conclusion.** Measurement literacy is foundational. Accuracy, precision, resolution and calibration should appear in field notes and reports. A modest instrument used transparently is more useful than an expensive instrument whose limitations are ignored.

## AERM Research and Reporting Checklist

Before publication, confirm that the report:
- States the question and the observation separately from any interpretation.
- Identifies the location and conditions at a level that respects privacy and safety.
- Records equipment, settings, placement and known limitations.
- Preserves original files and documents any processing.
- Describes controls, alternative explanations and unsuccessful tests.
- Reports uncertainty and avoids claims stronger than the evidence.
- Has permission for all identifiable recordings, quotations and images.

> **AERM Core Principle:** No anomaly is considered paranormal until environmental, psychological, technical, and scientific explanations have been systematically evaluated.
