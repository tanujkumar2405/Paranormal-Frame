# Pareidolia: Why Does the Human Brain See Faces and Figures in Random Patterns?
## Introduction

A stain on a wall resembles a face. A tree appears to have a figure standing beneath it. Two bright points in a dark window seem like eyes. These experiences can be vivid and sincere, but the perceived object may not exist in the way the observer first interprets it.

Pareidolia is the tendency to perceive a meaningful pattern—often a face or familiar object—in ambiguous or random sensory information. It is a normal feature of human perception, not evidence of poor intelligence or dishonesty.

For paranormal research, pareidolia matters because investigations frequently take place in low light, unfamiliar buildings and emotionally charged situations. Understanding it helps researchers distinguish what was seen from what was inferred.

## 1. How Pattern Recognition Works

The brain does not passively record the world like a camera. It processes incomplete sensory input, uses context and prior experience, and builds an interpretation that helps us act quickly.

Recognising faces is particularly important to social life. Humans are highly attentive to face-like arrangements, even when the features are crude or incomplete. Two dark marks above a horizontal line may be enough to suggest a face.

This sensitivity is useful in everyday life, but it can also produce false positives when the input is ambiguous.

## 2. Why Unfamiliar Locations Encourage Misinterpretation

Several conditions can make ambiguous patterns harder to identify:

- **Low illumination:** less visual detail reaches the observer.
- **Peripheral viewing:** peripheral vision is sensitive to change but provides less fine detail.
- **Brief exposure:** a fleeting glimpse may not provide enough information.
- **Expectation:** stories or suggestions can direct attention toward a particular interpretation.
- **Stress and fatigue:** may affect attention and judgement.
- **Visual complexity:** peeling paint, branches, fabric and irregular surfaces provide many possible patterns.

These factors do not prove that a reported figure was imagined. They identify conditions under which alternative interpretations should be examined.

## 3. Top-Down and Bottom-Up Processing

Bottom-up processing begins with sensory information. Top-down processing uses expectations, knowledge and context to interpret that information.

In a clear photograph, a face may be supported by detailed features. In a dark corridor, the visual input may be incomplete, so expectation can play a larger role. If an investigator has been told that a figure appears near a staircase, their attention may become especially sensitive to shapes in that location.

This is why witness interviews should begin with neutral questions. Asking "What did you notice?" is less leading than asking "Did you see the woman in white?"

## 4. A Practical Visual-Report Protocol

When someone reports a figure or face:

1. Ask the witness to describe the observation in their own words.
2. Record where they were standing, their viewing direction and approximate distance.
3. Document illumination, duration and whether the observation was direct or peripheral.
4. Ask what they saw before offering possible interpretations.
5. Photograph or video the scene from the original viewpoint, if safe and lawful.
6. Inspect the scene under documented lighting conditions.
7. Check for clothing, curtains, branches, reflections, stains, furniture and changing shadows.
8. Obtain independent accounts before witnesses discuss their interpretations.

Do not repeatedly suggest possible shapes to the witness. Repeated prompting can influence later reports.

## 5. A Simple Controlled Demonstration

A team can explore visual ambiguity without staging a frightening experience.

Photograph an ordinary textured surface under several lighting angles. Show the images in random order to volunteers who have not been told what the surface is. Ask each person to describe what they see, including "no recognisable object" as an acceptable response.

Compare independent descriptions. If people report different objects from the same image, that demonstrates interpretive variability. It does not establish that every report in a field investigation is pareidolia.

For stronger methodology, predefine the questions, use consistent image sizes and lighting, avoid revealing other participants' answers, and record uncertainty.

## 6. How to Report a Possible Visual Anomaly

Separate three layers:

- **Observation:** "A dark region approximately the height of the door was visible for two seconds."
- **Interpretation:** "The witness thought it resembled a standing person."
- **Assessment:** "The team inspected the doorway, lighting, reflections and camera footage; the source was not established."

This format respects the witness while preventing an interpretation from becoming an unverified fact.

## Conclusion

Pareidolia demonstrates how efficiently the human brain detects meaningful patterns—and why that ability can sometimes mislead us. In low-light environments, ambiguous shapes may resemble faces or figures, particularly when expectation and limited visual information influence interpretation.

AERM does not use pareidolia as a blanket explanation for unusual reports. It is one hypothesis to evaluate alongside lighting, reflections, camera artefacts, environmental conditions and independent evidence. The best investigation records the observation precisely, tests alternatives and remains open about uncertainty.

> **AERM principle:** No anomaly is considered paranormal until environmental, psychological, technical, and scientific explanations have been systematically evaluated.
