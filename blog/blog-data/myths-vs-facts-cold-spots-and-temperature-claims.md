# Does a Cold Spot Mean a Presence? Separating Temperature Claims from Thermal Evidence

A sudden sensation of cold is frequently described in paranormal stories as a sign that something has entered a room. Temperature changes are real and measurable, but the interpretation of a cold sensation requires more than a hand held in the air or a single thermometer reading.

Heat moves through conduction, convection and radiation. A draught can cool skin through convection; a cold wall can exchange radiant heat with a person; damp clothing can increase evaporative cooling. A person may therefore feel cold even when the room's air temperature changes very little. Localised airflow from gaps, vents, stairwells or poorly sealed windows can create small zones of discomfort.

A thermometer measures temperature at its sensor, not the entire room. Infrared cameras estimate surface temperature from emitted radiation and depend on emissivity and reflected-temperature settings. A reading from a shiny surface may be misleading. Slow sensors may also miss short-lived changes.

**Myth versus fact.** Myth: a cold sensation proves a nearby entity. Fact: human thermal sensation depends on air speed, humidity, clothing, radiant temperature and adaptation. Myth: an infrared camera sees cold air. Fact: most thermal cameras map surface radiation; they do not directly image airflow. Myth: one low reading is enough. Fact: a defensible claim needs a baseline, repeat measurements and instrument details.

**Field method.** Place at least two temperature loggers at fixed, documented positions—one near the reported location and one as a comparison point. Record air temperature, relative humidity and air movement. Note doors, windows, HVAC operation and people entering the room. If using a thermal camera, photograph the visible scene as well, document emissivity settings and verify important surface readings with a contact probe where appropriate. Repeat the measurements under comparable conditions.

Do not deliberately chill participants or expose them to unsafe environments. If a location is cold because of structural damage, poor weather protection or failed heating, address the building-safety issue first.

**Conclusion.** A cold spot is a useful environmental observation when it is measured properly. It is not a diagnosis of a presence. The responsible question is what changed, where, for how long, and whether the result can be reproduced.
