## Introduction

A corridor, a stairwell and a furnished bedroom can respond very differently to the same sound. A short hand clap may seem to linger in one room, disappear quickly in another, or return as a distinct echo from a distant wall. These differences are not mysterious in themselves: they arise from room geometry, surface materials, openings, furnishings and the frequency content of the sound.

Acoustic fingerprinting is the practice of measuring and describing those characteristics. In building acoustics and audio engineering, a room's response can be studied using an impulse response: a measurement of how the space changes a sound as it travels from a source to a microphone. Could this help investigators assess reports of unexplained knocks, voices or footsteps? Yes—as a way to document the acoustic environment and test ordinary explanations, not as a method for identifying a paranormal presence.

## What makes a room sound different?

When a sound is produced, some energy reaches the listener directly. Other energy reflects from walls, ceilings, floors, furniture and objects. The reflections arrive at different times and combine with the direct sound.

Several properties matter:

- **Room dimensions and geometry:** long corridors may produce delayed reflections; parallel hard surfaces can create flutter echoes.
- **Surface absorption:** curtains, carpets, upholstered furniture and acoustic panels absorb some sound energy.
- **Diffusion:** irregular surfaces scatter reflections in different directions rather than returning a strong, concentrated reflection.
- **Openings and connected spaces:** doors, windows, stairwells and ventilation ducts can allow sound to travel between areas.
- **Frequency:** a room may absorb high frequencies while allowing low-frequency energy to persist, changing the perceived character of a sound.

A room does not have one immutable signature. Its response changes when doors are opened, furniture is moved, people enter, or temperature and humidity vary.

## Impulse response and reverberation

An impulse response describes the output of a system after a very short input. In practice, engineers often use a calibrated measurement microphone and a test signal such as a logarithmic sine sweep. The recorded response can be processed to estimate the room's impulse response.

A common summary measure is reverberation time. **RT60** is the time required for sound energy to decay by 60 decibels after the source stops. In real rooms, the decay may not be a straight line, and measurements may estimate RT60 from a shorter decay range. The method and assumptions should therefore be reported.

Other useful descriptors include:

- **Early decay time (EDT):** relates to the initial part of the decay and can better reflect perceived reverberance in some situations.
- **Clarity measures:** compare early arriving sound energy with later reverberant energy.
- **Frequency-dependent decay:** shows whether low, middle and high frequencies behave differently.
- **Impulse-response plots:** can reveal discrete reflections, flutter echoes and delayed arrivals.

A clap is not a calibrated impulse source. It can be useful for a preliminary demonstration, but it should not be treated as a quantitative measurement.

## Can a room be acoustically fingerprinted?

A measurement set can help distinguish one recording position or room configuration from another under controlled conditions. A useful record might include room dimensions, source and microphone coordinates, microphone orientation, test-signal level, door and window states, furnishings, background noise and temperature.

However, “unique fingerprint” needs qualification. Different rooms can have similar reverberation times. The same room can produce different measurements when the microphone moves by a small distance. Microphone frequency response, automatic gain control, compression and background noise can also alter the result.

Acoustic measurements are therefore best understood as a **location profile**, not a universal identity tag. A claim that a recording came from a particular room should be supported by repeatable measurements and independent corroborating information.

## Application to unexplained audio reports

Suppose a team records what sounds like a whisper in a corridor. Before assigning meaning, investigators can ask:

1. Was another person speaking in an adjoining room?
2. Could an open doorway, ventilation duct or stairwell have carried the sound?
3. Does the room produce reflections that make distant speech seem close?
4. Does the sound recur when the same source and microphone positions are tested?
5. Does the event appear on more than one independently placed recorder?

A controlled playback can help test whether a known sound, played from a documented position, resembles the reported event at the recording point. Playback is not proof that the original event had that source; it tests plausibility.

## AERM field protocol

### Equipment

- Recorder capable of uncompressed WAV recording
- Measurement microphone with a documented frequency response, where available
- Calibrated audio interface or sound-level meter for quantitative work
- Measuring tape or laser distance meter
- Tripod and microphone stand
- Laptop with an established impulse-response or audio-analysis workflow
- Camera or field notebook for documenting room layout

### Procedure

1. Obtain permission and record the room's condition before moving anything.
2. Sketch the room, dimensions, openings, materials and connected spaces.
3. Mark source and microphone positions so they can be reproduced.
4. Record a baseline of ambient sound without speaking or moving.
5. Use a repeatable test signal at a safe, consistent level. Avoid sudden high-volume impulses.
6. Repeat measurements at several positions and, if relevant, with doors open and closed.
7. Preserve original recordings. Analyse working copies and record software, settings and processing steps.
8. Repeat the test on another occasion if the claim depends on a stable room characteristic.

### Controls and limitations

Do not compare measurements made with different microphones or automatic recording settings as though they were directly equivalent. Note wind, traffic, electrical hum, people and equipment noise. A room response can help explain why sounds are distorted or apparently displaced, but it cannot by itself identify the source of an unexplained sound.

## Interpretation

An acoustic anomaly is a measurement or sound that differs from an established baseline. It is not automatically evidence of an unusual agent. The investigator should distinguish between a perceptual impression (“it sounded like a voice”), an acoustic observation (“a transient occurred at 02:14:08”) and an interpretation (“the transient was a response”). Each step requires separate support.

## Conclusion

Acoustic fingerprinting offers a practical way to document how a location shapes sound. Impulse responses, reverberation measurements and repeatable recording positions can help test echoes, distant speech and apparent movement of sound. The method is valuable precisely because it replaces vague impressions with measurable conditions. Its limitations must remain visible: room responses vary, instruments colour recordings, and similar acoustic profiles do not prove a common source.
